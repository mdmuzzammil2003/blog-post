How much battery storage do you actually need?

Forget “how much can I afford?” — start with how much electricity your home really uses.

8 min read
Updated 2026
Expert reviewed
Sizing a battery around real usage — not guesswork.

Most people shopping for a home battery start with the wrong question. They ask, “How much can I afford?” instead of asking, “How much battery do I actually need?”

The right answer starts with something simple: how much electricity does your home use each day?

Start with your electricity bill — it tells the real story.

Start with your electricity use

Your electricity bill can tell you how much energy your home uses. Look at your daily or monthly usage and work out your average daily consumption.

An average Australian home may use around 16 kWh of electricity per day. But homes with air conditioning, electric hot water, a pool, or an EV can use much more.

Bigger homes need more storage

A larger home can easily use 30 kWh or more per day. Some large homes with several major appliances and EV charging can use 60 kWh or more.

This is why there is no single battery size that is right for every home. Your battery should match your actual electricity needs.

Evening and overnight — when your battery really shines.

Look at your night-time usage

Your battery is most useful when your solar panels are not producing electricity. This usually means during the evening, overnight, and early morning.

If you work during the day, much of your electricity may be used after the sun goes down. As a simple starting point, you could assume around 70% of your daily energy use happens outside solar-producing hours, but your real usage pattern may be very different.

Here’s a simple example: Imagine your home uses 16 kWh per day. If around 70% is used when your solar panels are not producing enough power, that’s about 11 kWh that may need to come from your battery or the grid. That gives you a useful starting point — but it doesn’t mean you automatically need an 11 kWh battery.

Usable capacity matters

A battery’s advertised capacity is not always the amount of energy you can actually use. The usable capacity is the amount of stored energy available to power your home.

For example, a battery may have a higher rated capacity but provide less energy for everyday use. Always check the usable capacity before comparing battery sizes.

Batteries also lose some energy

Energy is lost when electricity moves into and out of a battery. Good LFP batteries can have around 90–95% round-trip efficiency.

In simple terms, if you put 10 kWh into the battery, you may get around 9–9.5 kWh back. This is why it is useful to leave some extra capacity when sizing your battery.

Usable capacity & efficiency — two numbers that really matter.

How much battery do you need?

The right battery size depends on what you want your system to do. A small battery may be enough for essential appliances, while a larger battery can cover more of your home for longer.

What you want covered Usable battery capacity Typical situation
Essential appliances 5–10 kWh Fridge, lights, Wi‑Fi and a few outlets
Most of the home 10–20 kWh Evening use, time-of-use savings and broader backup
Near off-grid or long outages 25 kWh+ Large homes, heavy energy use or multi-day backup

These are starting points, not fixed rules. Your actual battery size should be based on your electricity use, solar generation, appliances and how much grid electricity you want to avoid.

Going nearly grid-free needs more planning

If your goal is to use almost no electricity from the grid, simply buying a large battery is not enough. You also need enough solar panels to recharge the battery during the day.

You also need to think about cloudy days, winter solar production, your inverter size, and how much electricity your home uses overnight.

What about sending power back to the grid?

You may produce more solar electricity than your home and battery can use. In that case, some systems can send the extra electricity back to the grid.

However, export limits and electricity plans can affect how much you can export. So, if exporting your surplus is part of your plan, check the rules for your local network and your electricity retailer.

Bigger is not always better

A huge battery may sound like the safest option, but it does not automatically make financial or practical sense. If your battery regularly stays half-full, you may be paying for storage that you rarely use.

The goal is not to buy the biggest battery you can afford. The goal is to buy the right amount of storage for the way your home actually uses electricity.

The simple way to think about it

Start with your average daily electricity use. Then look at how much electricity you use after your solar stops producing, because that is where your battery can make the biggest difference.

From there, consider your solar system, usable battery capacity, efficiency, heavy appliances, EV charging, backup needs and your goal for reducing grid use.

So, how much battery do you actually need?

For some Australian homes, 5–10 kWh may be enough for basic backup. Homes wanting to cover most of their evening use may look at 10–20 kWh, while homes aiming for near off-grid operation or longer backup may need 25 kWh or more.

The important thing is to size the battery around your home, your electricity use and your goal. A well-sized solar and battery system can help you use more of your own solar power, reduce your reliance on the grid, and potentially export your surplus when conditions allow.
